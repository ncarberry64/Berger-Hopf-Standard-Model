# What the Blackhole Tracker has demonstrated

BHSM frames the search as a hunt for gravitational topography on the hypersurface and for evidence that distinguishes different envelopments. The author calls the spacetime differential associated with an envelope boundary the **BHSM envelope-boundary differential**. This defined usage is separate from conventional rotational frame dragging. The paper relates it to its existing, explicitly referenced exterior curvature construction and derives a conditional compact-versus-extended bending-profile diagnostic. These are one common optical response counted once; they do not add another mass or force. Equal optical responses retain their degeneracy, and no new extended-halo classifier, global hypersphere radius, microscopic boundary or spin measurement is claimed. The [dated author definition](https://github.com/ncarberry64/Berger-Hopf-Standard-Model/blob/7ebc9f226e0d9abc68e2ec61c90a8b262081c733/docs/BHSM_TRACKER_ENVELOPMENT_SEARCH_CLARIFICATION_20261023.md) preserves the terminology and its research scope.

The current primary research target is **Gaia18ajz**, a previously published potential black hole. Its published parallax and lens-light analysis motivates investigation, but all eight model/prior/near--far alternatives remain visible, including lower-mass explanations. It is not a new tracker discovery. The known black-hole benchmark and the brown-dwarf comparison are separate controls; neither has been independently recovered by this engine.

RC6 adds a frozen evidence spectrum of **1,808 model records**, a measured-angular-track workspace and a reproducible public-data context helper. Model records are not a count of distinct objects or black holes. The spectrum currently has **zero admitted measured lens tracks**. The track software passes synthetic covariance and replay checks; those tests do not supply an observed trajectory or a mass. The 1,032-row public ZTF intake for Gaia18ajz remains unassociated context awaiting an independently reviewed native-data analysis. **Scientific publication remains held for that evidence review.**

The software reads the versioned OGLE survey brightness inputs directly. It keeps the original times, errors, instruments and source-row identities, runs a stated lensing model, checks the result, and saves an analysis that can be replayed. It does not need imaginary image positions or time delays.

All 100 selected historical OGLE-III events were processed, including every unsuccessful result. Their 76,344 brightness measurements and supplied raw files were preserved. Selection and file hashes were checked before fitting.

Most light curves need more than this simple model. Eighty-nine fail its photometric quality screen. Eleven pass that particular screen, although one screen does not resolve every sampling, blending, calibration or alternative-model concern.

Two historical events pass the stricter follow-up rules: OGLE3-ULENS-0585 and OGLE3-ULENS-3092, with fitted lensing timescales of about 268 and 188 days. Follow-up means checking archived data, testing better calibrated models, and assessing whether detailed imaging could separate the relevant stars. Their peaks are in the past; these are not current alerts.

**Neither is a newly identified black hole.** A slow event can mean a heavier lens, a different distance, or slower relative motion. These brightness curves alone cannot distinguish those explanations. Mass, distance and absolute lens motion are therefore unavailable.

The alias search matters. The second event was already observed as OGLE-2007-BLG-057 and MOA-2007-BLG-027 and appears in earlier work. The first is already a published OGLE catalog event. Bounded searches did not locate a specific earlier lens-interpretation paper for it, which does not prove novelty. Four other pilot events overlap a published parallax study, including an already discussed remnant candidate. Their associations are recorded.

The RC5 candidate atlas makes these directions useful without waiting for a precise mass. It joins all 100 pilot events with 59 published parallax events and counts four shared identities once: **155 distinct historical events**. Every failed or flagged pilot result stays visible. The app, command line and Python interface can filter the records, inspect the evidence and export a replay that retains the complete inventory.

The atlas has three investigation targets, with different reasons to investigate them. The two pilot followups keep their original screening qualifications. PAR-13 is a third, disputed literature target: one prior-dependent analysis suggests a black-hole-scale mass, but its Gaia source astrometry is unreliable and a different source-motion estimate permits a lower mass. The historical study's 13 dark-remnant-consistent events are not 13 black holes; the category also includes white dwarfs and neutron stars. A later reanalysis even favors a roughly 0.9-solar-mass star for PAR-02, once called the strongest black-hole candidate in that collection. The atlas keeps the changed interpretation rather than repeating an outdated claim.

**A sky marker is where the background source/event was cataloged, not a measured present-day black-hole position.** J2000 describes the catalog's coordinate equinox, not a supplied observation date. Lens mass, distance, position and absolute motion stay unavailable. The map cannot show reliable three-dimensional tracks or declare an unmarked patch free of black holes.

The known OGLE-2011-BLG-0462 black-hole system appears separately as a published benchmark, adding an optional 156th marker. Its strong identification is explicitly labeled as a literature result. Putting it on the map does not mean this engine has independently recovered its mass or classification. No atlas entry is a new black-hole identification.

The known black-hole system is a useful hard test. With the supplied **2022 version** of its observations, the program reads more than 61,000 ground-based brightness measurements. All five simple fits converge, yet all fail the declared scientific checks. A program can finish an optimization without explaining the data.

The Hubble table presents a separate obstacle. The exact conversion from its saved numeric times to observing epochs, and parts of its frame/calibration history, have not been recovered. Simply treating those numbers as Modified Julian Dates disagrees with the matching exposure windows. The program does not guess a date shift or apply an old filter correction twice. A reliable combined mass calculation requires verified conventions and an adequate joint model. Newer published reductions cannot silently replace the older benchmark data; the following 2023 analysis has a separate version and audit.

A separate 2023 observation version now makes a combined Hubble/OGLE calculation possible. The new app imports the real observations, fits angular deflection and annual parallax together, and replays the saved state. That is software progress, not a reliable mass result. Every one of the three frozen noise models still fails detailed residual checks. A conditional number near seven solar masses is displayed with a failure label and no accepted mass interval. The program has not independently established the known black hole's classification.

A different, carefully validated estimator may be possible, as may improved calibration or further observations. It cannot be certified by repeatedly changing the model until its answer matches the published mass. The saved audit explains the remaining correlation and influential-visit problems.

BHSM remains the interpretation. The ordinary field represents one effective gravitational exterior; its boundary description is counted once. When suitable observations identify mass, the same value can be expressed through a declared enclosing sphere's curvature or boundary energy. These survey-only fits do not identify mass, so they cannot identify those derived quantities either.

The dated BHSM core/surface proposal is recorded separately from measurements. Its abstract core is assigned no ordinary clock, trajectory or geometry. The reviewed mathematics states exactly when the simplified spherical membrane equation applies and how an applied pressure enters. The recovered original PDFs support their restricted calculations; they do not solve a complete gravitational interface, determine a lifetime or measure core dynamics.

Earlier synthetic demonstrations retain their own code versions and assumptions. They show useful mass/motion recovery from suitably rich resolved-image measurements in the tested model, without implying every survey light curve contains that information. The optional R1 envelope calculation exposes signals hidden by projection or ordinary shear; it is not an observed R1 deformation.

The original observational baseline delivers reproducible screening and two historical reanalysis priorities. The atlas extends the inventory and localization to 155 distinct events, preserves a third disputed investigation target and keeps a separate published benchmark. A stronger physical result still needs verified angular-scale/parallax data, calibration and crowded-field associations, an adequate combined likelihood, and evidence against ordinary competing explanations. A precise mass is not required to make investigation useful; generic lensing and long duration do not establish a black hole.
